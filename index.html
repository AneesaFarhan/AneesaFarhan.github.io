Seven cities in four years, and the unnamed feeling of living everywhere yet belonging nowhere.

I was born in Faisalabad, grew up in Lahore, and have spent my degree moving between San Francisco, Taipei, Seoul, Buenos Aires, Tokyo and Berlin. In most of them I have felt like I was catching up, with a standard of education everyone around me seemed to have arrived already holding. If it felt that way for me, with a good school behind me, I keep thinking about how much further that distance must run for almost everyone else. I work on AI systems for the places where that infrastructure thins out, or was never there at all.

01

An on-device clinical extraction pipeline

Children's National Hospital · Washington, DC · Jul – Sep 2026

Clinicians usually need a handful of specific facts from long, messy patient documents, but those facts are often buried across scanned charts and discharge summaries. This pipeline automates that first pass: upload a document, select what you need, and get each answer back with a citation to the page it came from. Data privacy was the main constraint shaping the system, so the entire pipeline runs locally and patient data stays within the local environment.

PyMuPDF4LLM extracts text directly from digital pages, while a vision OCR model handles the scans it can't. BM25 retrieval finds the relevant pages for each question, and a fine-tuned MedGemma 4B generates the answers from those pages. I also added automated cross-field and citation checks to catch hallucinated or misattributed values before they reach a clinician.

In testing, estimated manual review time dropped by about 60%. Six rounds of usability testing also helped resolve the citation-display and progress-tracking issues that were getting in the way of moving the system toward a clinical pilot.

02

A low-cost offline learning device for students in low-infrastructure schools

Capstone · Minerva University · In progress, Fall 2026 · Pakistan case study

A rugged device designed to function without continuous internet access, built for students in underserved communities preparing for university entrance exams. The device would prioritize storage over compute, support solar charging and a real power budget, and be developed under a cost-optimisation constraint. It would periodically sync with a paired device abroad to receive updated content.

The device would be designed for deployment across developing countries, with community-anchored distribution strategies adapted to each local education ecosystem. For the Pakistan case study, I plan to use agent-based simulation to examine distribution through the government, NGO, and UN channels already connected to schools, testing how these strategies actually perform against Pakistan's school-density and infrastructure data.

How it got here

The original proposal leaned toward healthcare education, largely because of my summer at Children's National. Conversations with my advisor, my work-study manager, and a friend running a structurally similar community-health-worker project in Pakistan kept pointing me toward the same underlying problem: what actually motivated me was geographic inequity in access to guidance, not healthcare itself. I weighed that against two other directions, a knowledge-diffusion simulation and a research-aggregation tool, and against the lessons from low-infrastructure hardware efforts like One Laptop Per Child. My main motivation was that, for most people, where they're born still determines what they get to learn, highlighting inequity in the current infrastructure despite the availability of existing resources.

03

ARAS, research-reasoning feedback for students without a lab to learn it in

CP192 · Minerva University · Spring 2026 · 5 iterations

Most students pick up how to frame a research question, state an assumption, or justify a method by watching someone else do it, in a lab or a well-resourced classroom. Students without that apprenticeship rarely get taught it explicitly anywhere. ARAS makes the invisible curriculum explicit. It breaks scientific reasoning into seven stages, from problem framing through to conclusion synthesis, and gives field-adaptive, rubric-grounded critique at each one.

The idea was partly shaped by the Minerva model, where I saw how much of learning could depend on the quality of the environment around you, and, at times, experienced what it meant not to have access to a lab. That connects to the broader question that keeps motivating my work: how much of a student's opportunity to learn depends on where they happen to be and what resources exist around them? ARAS is built around the assumption that students lacking those resources are not lacking ability; they are often lacking the same access to practice, feedback, and apprenticeship.

It is built specifically not to generate research on the student's behalf. The critique pushes back on weak reasoning instead of replacing it, so the student still has to do the thinking.

How it got here

Five iterations based on real feedback from a committee oral defence: tightening field-specificity so the critique does not read as generic, adding anti-hallucination grounding so feedback stays tethered to what the student actually wrote, benchmarking against model research proposals, and adding portable session profiles so progress carries across sessions.

Why

I was born in Faisalabad and educated in a sheltered, privileged pocket of Lahore. A good school, a stable home, parents who could absorb the cost of an international application. That still did not protect me from what it costs to apply from outside the systems an application quietly assumes you already belong to.

What stays with me is knowing how many students in Pakistan had far less than I did and were every bit as capable. The difference was not ability. It was whether someone was there to guide them, whether their family could afford the cost, and whether their school had the infrastructure to make what they were capable of visible beyond it. I went into computer science because I wanted to do something about that gap rather than accept it as the cost of where someone was born.

Where you're born still decides what you get to learn.

In my first week at university, I sat at Ocean Beach and made myself a promise: whatever I built here had to eventually make its way back to the people who did not get the same starting point I did. That promise has become less abstract with every project since. I am interested in using AI where geography becomes a constraint by building systems that can carry some of those resources students lack across the gap.

I used to think I would eventually out-think the tools I was using. I was proven wrong within a few weeks of using one seriously, and I have come to think that is the more useful place to argue from. The people who close this gap will not be the ones who outperform AI. They will be the ones who know exactly where to point it and who make sure the students who were never given the opportunity to learn that can access it too.
